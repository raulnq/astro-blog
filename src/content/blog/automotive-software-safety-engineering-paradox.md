A provocative question recently debated on the Modern Software Engineering channel asked if developers would drive cars if they were only as safe as software. While the initial gut reaction for many software professionals might be a resounding 'no,' the discussion quickly revealed a nuanced reality: modern vehicles are already heavily reliant on complex software, meaning 'yes, we do.' The conversation highlighted a critical distinction between safety-critical systems, such as engine management, and consumer-facing infotainment software. Historically, pioneering figures like Margaret Hamilton, who coined 'software engineering' for the Apollo program, championed rigorous, 'man-rated' development to ensure systems would 'fail safe'—a principle still underpinning high-stakes applications. However, the application of these standards varies significantly, with infotainment systems often leveraging less scrutinized consumer-grade software.

Despite the perceived rigor, the discussion revealed that traditional 'safety-critical' processes can sometimes be an 'excuse for expense,' leading to outdated methodologies that do not necessarily guarantee higher quality. Examples cited included Toyota's software-induced runaway acceleration issues, characterized by '10,000-line god functions,' and the Arianne 5 overflow bug, suggesting that comprehensive system testing, rather than internal code quality, often serves as the ultimate safeguard. Experts argued for adopting modern software engineering principles universally, emphasizing rapid development cycles, continuous feedback, static analysis, and a 'design for failure' mindset—accommodating edge cases like NaN values or ambiguous signal states. The consensus underscored that elevating software quality across all domains, whether automotive or otherwise, hinges on proactive engineering practices and a culture that embraces continuous improvement and robust verification, rather than an illusion of safety based on legacy processes.
